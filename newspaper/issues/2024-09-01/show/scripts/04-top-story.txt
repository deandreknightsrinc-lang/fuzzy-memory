APOSTLE SIR DEANDRE KNIGHT:
The Magic Spell Hypothesis. Researchers tested their hypothesis by asking 200 non-lawyers to write laws and stories about crimes. The participants naturally used convoluted structures when writing laws prohibiting crimes such as drunk driving, burglary, arson, and drug trafficking, but opted for plain English in their fiction stories. The pattern held true even when participants were asked to revise their drafts. Instead, the study supports what the researchers call the "magic spell hypothesis." Just as spells are distinguished by a unique style that sets them apart from everyday language, legalese seems to convey a similar sense of formality and power. "People seem to understand that there's an implicit rule that this is how laws should sound, and they write them that way," says Edward Gibson, an MIT professor of brain and cognitive sciences and the senior author of the study. In a 2022 study, the same team examined 3.5 million words from legal contracts, comparing them with movie scripts, newspaper articles, and academic papers. Their analysis uncovered a common pattern in legal documents: lengthy definitions often interrupt sentences, a structure known as "center-embedding." In another series of experiments, around 80 participants were asked to draft laws and then write descriptions explaining those laws to visitors from another country. When crafting the laws they consistently used center-embedded structures, but avoided that phrasing in the explanations. This switching points to the idea that legalese is more about signaling authority than giving clarity. Death and life are in the power of the tongue.
